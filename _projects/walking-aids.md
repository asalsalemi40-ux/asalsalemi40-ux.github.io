<div class="chapter">
<h2>The problem</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
About 30 percent of people over 65 fall at least once a year, and some of those falls end in a broken hip or wrist and a long loss of independence. I focused on one situation: walking in low light.

With age, balance leans more on eyesight, because the inner ear and the sense of where the body is grow weaker. At night many older people get up without switching on the main light, so as not to wake up fully, and cross a dark room to the bathroom: sleepy, unsteady, and unable to see a step, the edge of a rug or a cable.
</div>
</div>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>What the research showed</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
I searched PubMed, Google Scholar and open-access journals for studies on night-time falls, lighting, eyesight and walking aids, and kept peer-reviewed sources, from 2020 on wherever I could.

- In a study of 12 care homes, 57.7 percent of bedrooms and 68.5 percent of bathrooms were lit below the level recommended for walking and bathing, and dimmer rooms went with more falls.
- In a small trial, LED strips around bathroom door frames cut night-time falls by 34 percent compared with ordinary night lights.
- Many falls happen on the same short route, from bed to bathroom, at night.
- Using a cane can lower the risk of falling by up to about 25 percent. Yet only about 10 percent of people over 65 in Iran use one: for many, holding a cane says "I'm frail".
- Complicated smart devices meet resistance. A simple object people can use every day is more likely to be used at all.
</div>
</div>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>Design requirements</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
1. Light on the path, without glare and without switching on the room lights
2. Steady support that improves stability and balance
3. A simple interface: one large button, one action
4. Light weight, and a grip that suits older hands
5. Safe, durable parts, and feet that don't slip

I also checked the design against ISO 11334 (requirements and strength tests for walking aids) and ISO 24415 (tips): a positive height lock, joints that can't work loose under load, and rubber feet that grip on wet floors.
</div>
</div>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>Exploration</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
Standard walkers are cheap and familiar, but wheeled models are less stable, curbs and car doors are hard, and lifting the frame tires weak hands and painful shoulders. Research I reviewed puts the best handle height at about 48 percent of body height. I first explored a family of walker frames, each answering a different need, before narrowing down.
</div>
</div>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>Narrowing down</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
Two ideas kept coming back across the family: light for dark rooms and a base that stands on its own. The night trip to the bathroom needs both, and it needs them in something small enough to keep beside the bed. That became the final design.
</div>
</div>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>Final design: Night Cane</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
One object that does both jobs: a cane for support and a light for seeing the way.

- **Light in the handle.** A warm, soft LED in the front of the handle lights the floor a step or two ahead, so the edge of a step or a rug shows, without dazzling anyone.
- **One large button.** It sits under the thumb and needs very little force. A later version could add a motion sensor that switches the light on by itself.
- **A grip for older hands.** An offset, palm-shaped handle in a soft-touch finish eases the pressure on the wrist and doesn't slip.
- **Telescopic shaft.** Light aluminium that adjusts to the user's height and locks with a pin.
- **Four rubber feet.** A wider base that steadies each step and lets the cane stand on its own, so there's no bending down to pick it up.
</div>
</div>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>A night, step by step</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
The cane is designed around the moment it matters most.
</div>
</div>

<ol class="journey four">
<li><h3>Awake at 3 a.m.</h3><p>The bathroom is across a dark room, and switching on the ceiling light would mean waking up fully.</p></li>
<li><h3>One press</h3><p>Still sitting on the bed, a press of the thumb turns the light on.</p></li>
<li><h3>Walk with light</h3><p>Warm light marks the floor ahead while the four feet steady each step.</p></li>
<li><h3>Arrive</h3><p>At the bathroom door, one press turns it off. The cane stands on its own until it's needed.</p></li>
</ol>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>A box that's easy to open</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
The packaging follows the same rules as the product, for older hands and eyes:

- Large, high-contrast type and clear icons
- Opens without force or fine finger movements, with a raised mark that shows where
- A sturdy carton with a carry handle, and an insert that holds the cane still
- A one-page picture guide: set the height, switch on the light, check the rubber feet
- Plain warnings: careful on wet floors, replace the feet now and then, check the battery
</div>
</div>

<div class="chapter">
<h2>Designing for acceptance</h2>
<div class="body" markdown="1">
In Iran a cane can feel like a label of weakness, even though in Safavid and Qajar times a fine cane was a mark of status. So the cane should read as a personal accessory rather than medical equipment: a calm, friendly form, a choice of colours, nothing that looks futuristic.

The next step is testing the button and the height lock with older users who have weak fingers or a tremor, and adjusting both until they need no explanation.
</div>
</div>
